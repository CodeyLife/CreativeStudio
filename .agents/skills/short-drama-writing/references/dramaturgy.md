# Short-Drama Dramaturgy: Episode Hooks, Emotion Cadence, and Cut Points

The directing layer (Shot Equation, rhythm, light) lives in `cinematography.md`; the prose-to-segment contract lives in `adaptation-principles.md`. This file adds the **dramaturgy layer** — how a sequence of 10-15s segments becomes a short-drama episode that holds attention: when to hook, when to release, and where to cut.

All patterns below are genre-neutral narrative structures; they are derived from widely repeated short-drama craft practice and must be instantiated from the committed prose, never invented beyond it.

**Positioning: this is a selection-and-cut overlay, not story creation.** In chapter mode the story is already committed — reversals, plants, and opponent pressure are the prose's facts; the storyboard only presents them and cuts where they land. In idea mode there is no plot to build — a tens-of-seconds piece presents a situation or imagery strikingly. The devices below (releases, plants, opponent credibility) exist to verify that the material's own drama is fully and correctly carried into frames — never to fabricate drama the material lacks.

## 0. Scale Discipline (rules bind by length)

Short-drama output spans very different lengths, and series-scale devices do not survive compression. Apply every rule below by regime — and by mode: rules about conflict, nodes, releases, plants, and opponents bind only **conflict-carrying material**; spectacle pieces (visual shorts with no character conflict) follow the visual-progression arc described after the table and swap those devices for progression checks:

| Regime | Typical size | What binds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Micro (1-2 segments) | ~10-30s standalone | Conflict pieces: open inside the conflict; one beat; one release; end on the strongest sting. Spectacle pieces: signature subject, one progression step, end on the strongest frame. Rotation, plant distribution, opponent ladders, and cadence arithmetic do not apply. |
| Short (3-6 segments) | ~45-90s standalone | Conflict pieces: one main suppression→release arc — the single release resolves the strongest pressure the piece builds; at most one visible plant before any reveal; exits chosen (not rotated) among the hook types. Spectacle pieces: a 3-4 step progression ending on the fullest revelation. |
| Episode (7+ segments) | ~2-5 min chapter script | Full cadence (node every 2-4 segments), release-type rotation, multi-plant distribution for major reversals, opponent-credibility checks. |
| Serialized (multi-episode) | chapter series / author-planned run | Serial-layer attention waveform and hook configuration (`serial-rhythm.md`); placement decisions belong to the author/pipeline. |

The suppression→release mechanism itself is scale-invariant — even a 15-second clip can raise stakes in two or three seconds and pay them off once — but never stretch series-scale apparatus (rotation, multi-plant spreads, escalation ladders, payoff escalation) into pieces too short to carry them; in a micro or short piece that padding is watermark, not structure. Spectacle-mode pieces have no suppression→release at all: their arc is visual progression (see SKILL.md, idea mode).

## 1. The Attention Contract

Vertical short-drama viewers decide within the first seconds whether to keep watching. An episode built from 10-15s segments therefore obeys:

- **The first segment opens inside the conflict, not before it.** No morning routine, no scenery-establishing slow build. If the prose opens quietly, find the first clash inside the chapter and let the segment start on its brink; earlier context is presented later through flashback, dialogue, or on-screen text (see `adaptation-principles.md` §2).
- **The golden first five seconds, as ticks to hit:** around the 1-second mark, an image that strikes or a question that poses itself; by the 3-second mark, the core clash of the moment or a visible status gap; by the 5-second mark, the viewer holds an unanswered question. The runtime contract bounds the softer form: the core conflict, who opposes whom, and the protagonist's immediate goal must all be visible or spoken within the first ~10 seconds of the episode. Not the backstory — the current trouble.
- **Opening hook shapes** (choose what the prose actually supports):
  1. *Direct conflict* — confrontation, betrayal, or public humiliation already in frame on frame one. To land, the disparagement must be sharp enough to raise the viewer's blood before any reversal arrives.
  2. *Strong suspense* — an object, message, or action that poses an unanswered question (a document slammed on a table, an unrecognized caller).
  3. *Extreme contrast* — a status reversal the frame itself shows (honored guest turning outcast within one segment); the wider the gap between how the character is treated and what the frame hints they really are, the harder the later release lands.
  4. *Identity gap* — the character's shown status visibly mismatched with hints of a hidden one.
  5. *Deadline pressure* — a stated time limit or approaching consequence with visible stakes.
  6. *Start at the edge of a point of no return* — open at the moment the story's decisive turn begins (the acceptance, the return, the discovery), with the plan or its first consequence visible; the viewer needs to know that the character knows, and that action has started.
  7. *In medias res outcome* — open on a striking result (a verdict, a wedding, a confrontation already underway), then step back to earlier events; the result must read as implausible or contrary, so the viewer must know how it came to be. Backstep context arrives through presentation channels (`adaptation-principles.md` §2), never as narrated summary.

Spectacle pieces use none of the conflict shapes above: their opening is the signature subject at its most striking, and the "question" the opening raises is visual — what is this, how big is it, what am I actually looking at — not narrative. For spectacle and humor idea-mode pieces, the attention engine, the single subversion, and the one signature frame are designed upstream of this overlay (`attention-hooks.md`); the dramaturgy layer below binds them only where the idea itself states a character conflict (engine `conflict`).

## 2. Emotion Cadence

Short drama is emotion delivery, not exposition. The working loop is **suppress → release**, alternating:

- An **emotion node every 20-60 seconds** — that is, every 2-4 segments at the 10-15s budget (episode regime and up; micro/short spend one node, and spectacle pieces have none) — expressed as a dialogue clash, a physical action clash, or an information reveal. If three consecutive segments carry no node, the cadence is broken.
- **The depth of suppression sets the height of release** — build pressure (humiliation, dread, longing, an assumption hardening), then release it; a release with no preceding pressure reads as cheap and does not compound. The release must resolve the specific pressure that was built (the withheld proof is shown, the insult is answered in kind); releasing a different pressure than the one built breaks the circuit.
- **Release type taxonomy** (generic; instantiate from prose, never invent beyond it):
  - *Face-slap reversal* — the party who slighted or schemed is publicly answered with their own words, scheme, or standard turned back on them; the more composed the protagonist, the harder it lands; bystander reactions and the offender's visible cost are part of the release, not garnish.
  - *Crushing status reveal* — a hidden identity, resource, or capacity shown at ceremonial weight (with a witness scale: one person, one room, one crowd, or the whole world — the smaller the scale, the smaller the release).
  - *Counterattack comeback* — from a low point to a visible win, in steps rather than one leap, with witnesses who previously dismissed the protagonist.
  - *Emotional burst* — long-suppressed feeling breaks out: confession, accusation, reconciliation, collapse; body language carries it and the line stays plain and speakable, not literary.
  - *Revelation* — a held mystery resolves into a truth that reframes earlier scenes; it must be both unexpected and retroactively inevitable (planted, §5).
- **Rotate and escalate releases** (episode regime and up): the same release type twice in a row numbs; three times kills it. Each release should out-scale the previous one of its kind, and a big release needs small releases before it. Do not cancel a release immediately after granting it (the "sike" reversal) more than sparingly — done often, it reads as cheating the viewer. Micro and short regimes have no rotation: one release, spent on the strongest pressure.
- The **first release lands early**: within roughly the first third of the episode, at least one small reversal (a fact shown, an assumption overturned, evidence revealed) must detonate. All releases trace back to beats planted earlier — see §5.
- Peaks are distributed, not stacked: one peak per segment (the existing rhythm rule) and not every segment at maximum intensity; a deliberate brake segment is legitimate and makes the next release land harder.

## 3. Segment Exits Are Hooks

Every segment's exit state should pull the viewer into the next one. The strongest exit is a **question or raised stake**, not a settled afterglow:

- Cut **on the impact point** — the instant of reveal, contact, or decision — not after it resolves. The reveal itself may open the next segment.
- **Exit-hook types** (generic, instantiated from prose — rotate so consecutive exits do not repeat the same mechanism):
  1. *Identity suspense* — who this really is, about to surface.
  2. *Expectation reversal* — the last frame overturns what the viewer was led to assume.
  3. *Emotional suspension* — cut at the peak of a feeling, just before its consummation (the near-touch, the almost-confession, the tear that is about to fall); the interrupting event must be plausible, not arbitrary.
  4. *Half-revealed information* — part of a state-changing fact shown, the decisive remainder withheld (a torn page, a name half-read, a message cut off).
  5. *Incoming crisis* — danger or a deadline suddenly live, the protagonist's reaction not yet possible.
- Two mechanisms may combine on a strong exit (suspense + feeling, reversal + crisis); three at once overload the viewer. Never stack the same type twice — two reversals on one exit dilute each other.
- **A hook is a promise.** The next segment (or the next episode's opening) must acknowledge the exact question the hook raised — give new information that answers or complicates it; never resolve it in passing as if it had not been raised, and never dissolve a previous segment's hook within seconds as if it were nothing. That reads as a false promise and drains trust in every later hook.
- The **final segment of an episode ends on the strongest hook available** in the chapter material — the cliffhanger. Viewers should leave hungry, not satisfied. Series-level paid cut points sit at the same kind of pre-reveal instant; the storyboard's job is to supply cut-worthy moments, the placement decision belongs to the author/pipeline.

## 4. Dialogue and Presentation Density

- Every spoken line performs at least one of: **identity/relationship confirmation, conflict ignition, or consequence statement**. Lines that do none of these are filler — drop or compress them.
- Colloquial, short, speakable lines; no written-style prose or long monologues. Faithfulness rules of `adaptation-principles.md` §6 still apply: compression yes, fact rewriting no.
- **Silent-viewing safety**: key emotional beats must be readable from faces, actions, and on-screen text even with sound off; the strongest line of an exchange can double as a visible on-screen text where plausible (a message on a screen, a letter, a sign).
- Presentation over reaction: information arrives via flashback frames, spoken statements, or readable text — never via a reaction shot alone.

## 5. Reversals Need Plants

A reversal with no earlier plant is invention, not dramaturgy. The chain is **plant → overlook → detonate**:

- While enumerating beats, mark beats that can serve as plants (an object detail, a passing line, a prop with history) and make sure at least one *presentation channel* carries the plant visibly — a close-up insert, a spoken mention, a readable detail.
- When a later segment detonates a reversal, verify its plant exists among earlier beats of the chapter (or of earlier chapters, when adapting serialized material). If missing, the prose did not support it: do not fabricate.
- **Plant distribution for major reversals** (hidden identity, disguised motive — episode regime and up): spread at least three traceable plants across the episode's thirds — an early one that looks innocuous, a middle one that has a plausible in-scene explanation when it occurs, a late one that nearly gives the game away before being interrupted. Each plant must survive hindsight: on re-view, every "coincidence" points the same way. If the material supports only one plant, downgrade the reversal to a minor surprise or drop it; in micro and short regimes, one solid visible plant before the reveal is the whole requirement.
- A detonated reversal needs its **afterglow of shock on screen** — the hearer's visible recalibration, not just the information itself — and the revelation may open a further question to pull the story forward.

## 6. Character Economy

- Per episode, keep the **core triangle on screen**: protagonist, opponent, helper; a handful of speaking roles total. More named on-screen characters than a small handful dilutes recognition inside a vertical frame. The opening segment carries the fewest — no more than a couple of on-screen speakers — so the first images stay legible.
- Fast character establishment follows **label + contrast + secret**: one visual/social label (readable at a glance), one contrast against that label, and one hidden fact planted for later detonation. Anchors (a distinctive prop, a garment, a scar) reuse the shared-subject system so the label stays identical across segments.

### 6.1 Opponent pressure

The opponent is the engine of every release (§2); flat pressure makes flat payoffs. When beats are planned, the material should answer three questions about each opposing force — if it does not, flag the gap rather than inventing the answer:

1. **Motive** — why they oppose, statable in one line (interest, envy, fear, conviction). Opposition without motive reads as arbitrary and the viewer stops fearing it.
2. **Capability** — what makes them dangerous (resources, position, skill, information). Pressure must plausibly outweigh the protagonist's current means, or the clash carries no tension.
3. **Restraint** — why they have not simply crushed the protagonist already (dismissal, caution, waiting for a better moment, needing something from them). Without it, the antagonist's patience looks like a plot convenience.

Pressure **escalates in layers** — serialized-regime material only: a minor everyday irritant (defeated lightly, exits cleanly) should leave a hint of the stronger opponent behind it; the mid-tier opponent — the one who can genuinely hurt the protagonist — should win at least once in longer material to earn the low point the comeback pays off. Each dispatched layer hands off to the next ("you think that was the real threat?") rather than to silence. A single short piece (micro/short regime) has one opponent and no ladder: make that one pressure credible and leave escalation to the material's own sequel hooks.

Opponent dialogue registers scale with the tier: bottom-tier pressure speaks bluntly and openly, mid-tier speaks implicitly and transactionally, top-tier speaks unhurriedly from above the fight; a concealed opponent speaks warmly until the reveal flips the register (planted per §5). Instantiate registers from the prose's actual lines — do not restyle them.

## 7. Mapping to the 10-15s Segment Contract

| Dramaturgy concept | Segment-level realization |
| --- | --- |
| Opening hook | Peak of segment 1, landing within its first seconds |
| Emotion node | A segment whose peak is the node (clash, reveal, or decision) |
| Suppress → release | Alternating segment peaks; brake segments between releases |
| Exit hook | The exit state of each segment (the state the next segment inherits) |
| Cliffhanger / cut point | The final segment's exit — cut at the impact instant |
| Plant | A beat presented via insert shot, spoken line, or readable text in an earlier segment |
| First release | A reversal segment within the first third of the episode's segment sequence |

## 8. Failure Classes

| Symptom | Root cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Episode opens with scenery/routine | Opening beat chosen before the conflict | Start on the brink of the chapter's first clash; backfill context via flashback/dialogue/text |
| Flat middle; viewers drift | Emotion cadence broken (no node for 3+ segments) | Re-map peaks so a clash/reveal lands every 2-4 segments; add a brake, not filler |
| Release lands flat | No pressure built before it, no on-screen witnesses, or no aftermath | Stage the pressure beat first, add witness reactions and the offender's visible cost; never fake pressure the material lacks |
| Same release or hook type repeats 3+ times | Type fatigue | Rotate to another release/hook type; escalate intensity instead of repeating |
| Previous exit's hook ignored | Payoff contract broken | Open the next segment acknowledging the hook; answer or complicate it with new information |
| Hook deflates instantly next segment | False suspense | Give the answer room to land (a segment of consequences), then raise the next question from it |
| Reversal feels unearned | Plant never presented earlier | Insert the plant into an earlier segment via a presentation channel, or drop the reversal |
| Opponent reads arbitrary | Motive / capability / restraint unanswerable from the material | Flag the gap; instantiate only what the prose supports, or soften the opponent's role |
| Episode ends on a settled afterglow | Exit hook missing | Move the cut to the impact instant; let the reveal open the next episode |
| Dialogue exchanges feel like filler | Lines carry no identity/conflict/consequence | Compress to the lines that do; keep spoken timeline faithful |
| Too many characters to track | Character economy ignored | Fold minor roles into the triangle; give remaining labels visual anchors |
