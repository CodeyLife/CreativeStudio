# Serial Rhythm: Multi-Episode Attention and Fatigue Control

**Scope: serialized runs only.** Everything in this file presupposes a multi-episode series (a chapter sequence adapted as episodes, or an author-planned multi-part run). It does **not** apply to a single 10-180s short (micro/short regimes in `dramaturgy.md` §0) — a standalone short has one arc, one release, and one final sting, and none of the waveforms, hook configurations, or fatigue controls below exist at that size.

Companion to `dramaturgy.md` (the single-episode layer); this file covers the **series layer** — how attention, pressure, and payoffs are distributed across many episodes. All ratios are planning heuristics instantiated from the actual material; the committed prose/chapter sequence is the source of truth, and placement decisions belong to the author/pipeline.

## 1. Every episode opens as if it were the first

- Non-first episodes spend their first moments acknowledging the previous episode's hook (§3 of `dramaturgy.md` — a hook is a promise), then immediately raise fresh tension. "First reply, then new fire" — never a cold recap, never a slow re-establish.
- Recaps live in on-screen text or a single flashback frame at most; they never occupy a segment by themselves.

## 2. The four-phase waveform (percent-of-series, not fixed counts)

Series-level tension follows **tighten → release → tighten harder** in four phases, expressed as fractions of total episodes so the shape scales to any length:

| Phase | Span (approx.) | Rhythm character | Payoff scale |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Setup | first ~15% | Fast information density; small wins previewed | Small releases, cheap and frequent |
| Escalation | ~15-45% | Steady climb; opponent pressure grows; feelings complicate | Medium releases; "two steps forward, one step back" |
| Storm | ~45-80% | Fastest rhythm; reversals dense; the protagonist's low point | Large releases; the lowest low sits here |
| Resolution | final ~20% | All-out push to the summit, then deliberate deceleration | The largest release; final segments give afterglow, not hooks |

- Highs must **alternate with brakes**; a series that is uniformly intense numbs the viewer as surely as a flat one.
- The **single largest release belongs to the resolution phase** — a peak that arrives early leaves the rest of the series fighting an audience that has already been satisfied.
- The low point is real but short: two or three episodes of defeat at most before the comeback begins; the comeback's witnesses are the people who dismissed the protagonist earlier.

## 3. Hook and payoff configuration across a series

- Every episode ends on a hook except the final one, which closes with earned afterglow.
- Hook **types rotate** across episodes (the five types of `dramaturgy.md` §3); the same type three episodes running breeds immunity.
- The **strongest hook of the free span sits at its last episode** — the pre-reveal instant — because that is where the viewer decides whether to continue at all.
- A hook planted at an episode boundary must be honored in the next episode's opening (the promise rule); a series that fakes suspense once teaches viewers to stop trusting it.
- Release **intensity escalates** across the series within each release type, and release **types rotate** to prevent fatigue; between large releases, insert small ones and emotional breathers — but breathers still carry new information.

## 4. Fatigue and information hygiene

- No multi-episode stretch without material plot advancement; if a span produces no new facts, decisions, or relationship shifts, remap beats rather than inventing side events.
- The opening span establishes the core cast (a small handful), the protagonist's trouble, and at least a few open questions; it does not spend its biggest secret.
- Opponent pressure escalates in layers across the series (see `dramaturgy.md` §6.1): each dispatched layer hands off to a larger one, with a planted hint before each new layer appears.
- Major hidden-hand reversals plant at least three traceable beats spread across the series' spans (§5 of `dramaturgy.md`), each surviving hindsight.

## 5. Self-check for a span of episodes

- Does the span's tension waveform actually rise and fall (plot the segment peaks across episodes)?
- Is there any episode whose exit hook was never answered?
- Is the largest release of the span positioned where the material's own climax sits?
- Would a viewer who drops at any episode boundary feel an unresolved pull rather than a settled ending?
