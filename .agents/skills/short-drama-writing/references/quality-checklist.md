# Storyboard Quality Checklist

Run this after drafting a storyboard and before delivery. Each item is a question with a concrete pass condition. The pipeline's automated hints cover the mechanical subset (cut-point timing and spoken-line budget); this checklist is the full directing and dramaturgy pass (episode-layer rules expand `dramaturgy.md`; multi-episode spans add the serial-layer checks of `serial-rhythm.md` §5).

## 1. Coverage

- [ ] Every story beat (memory, setup, hook, decision, action, exchange) is carried by at least one segment.
- [ ] Every information block is *presented* — flashback frames, spoken lines, or on-screen text — not just reacted to.
- [ ] No segment exists without a beat; no invented plot exists beyond the prose.

## 2. Shot density, picture design and rhythm

- [ ] Every `[Shot N]` states framing (shot size, and angle when meaningful).
- [ ] Every shot states composition: where the subject sits, plus at least one of foreground occlusion / lead-in line / layer separation / negative space with a job.
- [ ] Every shot names its camera behavior (motion type per the H3 grammar, or a deliberate static hold).
- [ ] Every shot has a light/atmosphere element with a real highlight-shadow falloff (source, direction, or airborne particle).
- [ ] Every shot names a dominant colour and an accent, and says which element carries the accent.
- [ ] Anti-flat pass: no centred bare medium shot, no flat even light, no neutral frame, no constant-speed motion. Any hit is rewritten, not decorated with extra detail.
- [ ] The opening style line is reconstructable (focal feel, medium, lighting system, colour base), not a generic "cinematic" tag.
- [ ] No segment names the aspect ratio or orientation (no ratio digits, no "vertical/horizontal frame" wording) — the ratio is a generation parameter; vertical reading is carried by framing, camera height, and blocking instead.
- [ ] Each segment has one rhythm peak and one brake; the pattern differs across segments (not five identical builds).
- [ ] Shot 1 of the chapter (or segment) opens with an establishing beat; the last shot of each segment exits toward the next.
- [ ] Signature moments (where the prose peaks) use the build → peak → shock → afterglow pattern; no fabricated spectacle elsewhere.

## 2b. Dramaturgy (episode layer)

First place the piece in a regime (`dramaturgy.md` §0): micro (1-2 segments) / short (3-6) / episode (7+) / serialized run. Items marked ⚖ **scale** bind only in the regimes named; unmarked items bind everywhere. Items marked ◆ **conflict-only** bind only when the material carries a character conflict (chapter mode, or an idea that states a tension); spectacle pieces — organized as visual progression — swap those items for the §2 visual-progression checks plus cinematography.md §9 plus the §2d attention-design items, and no conflict requirement is imposed on them.

- [ ] ◆ The first segment opens inside the conflict; no routine or scenery-first build. *(Spectacle: opens on the most striking image of the signature subject — which is a legitimate, expected opening, not a failure.)*
- [ ] ◆ The opening ticks land: an image that strikes or a question that poses itself around the 1-second mark, the core clash or status gap by the 3-second mark, an open question in the viewer's hands by the 5-second mark. *(Spectacle: the 1-second tick is the same; the 3s/5s ticks become the signature subject visible and its scale/idea legible.)*
- [ ] ◆ ⚖ (episode+) An emotion node (clash, reveal, or decision) lands every 2-4 segments; no three consecutive flat segments. Micro/short: at least one node beats one. *(Spectacle: one progression step per shot replaces cadence arithmetic.)*
- [ ] ◆ Each node resolves the pressure it built (suppression → release); the release is staged with witnesses and aftermath where the material supports them. Micro/short: one release, resolving the piece's strongest pressure.
- [ ] ◆ ⚖ (episode+) Release and hook types vary across the episode; no type repeats three times in a row; a granted release is not instantly cancelled. Micro/short: exits are chosen among the hook types, not rotated. *(Spectacle: the final exit is the strongest frame, not a conflict hook.)*
- [ ] ◆ The first small reversal lands within the first third of the episode, and traces to a beat presented earlier (plant → overlook → detonate).
- [ ] ◆ ⚖ (episode+) Major reversals have at least three traceable plants spread across the episode, each surviving hindsight. Micro/short: one solid visible plant before the reveal.
- [ ] Every segment's exit raises a question or the stakes (conflict) or advances the progression's anticipation (spectacle); the final segment cuts on the impact instant or the strongest frame, not the afterglow.
- [ ] ◆ The next segment acknowledges the previous segment's hook; no hook is dropped or dissolved without new information.
- [ ] ◆ Every spoken line confirms identity, ignites conflict, or states consequence; filler lines are compressed out; the peak line lands on the shot that shows it.
- [ ] Key emotional beats are readable with sound off (faces, actions, on-screen text). *(Spectacle: the key image is legible with sound off.)*
- [ ] On-screen speaking cast stays within the core triangle plus a small handful; the opening segment carries the fewest; character labels are visually anchored. ◆ (Opponent side of the triangle is conflict-only.)
- [ ] ◆ Opposing pressure is answerable from the material (motive, capability, restraint) or the gap is flagged, not invented. ⚖ Escalation ladders (serialized only) are never asserted inside a standalone short.
- [ ] The piece's apparatus matches its regime — no rotation, plant spread, or waveform demanded of a piece too short to carry it; no conflict device demanded of a piece with no conflict.

## 2c. Adversarial checks (run against your own draft)

- [ ] "The page-one test" *(conflict material)*: would the first five seconds survive a cold viewer with zero context — no prior scene, no title card, no patience? A quiet establish or a waking-up routine fails. *(Spectacle pieces: the same test reads "is the most striking image on screen immediately" — a plain, undifferentiated establishing view of the subject fails, a composed striking one passes.)*
- [ ] "The generic-output test": swap your staged release for the most predictable version of itself (a shout instead of a calm counter, an insult answered by a louder insult). If it still describes your draft, the staging is not yet earning its peak — instantiate the material's own specifics.
- [ ] "The fake-suspense test": re-read each exit hook and ask what the next segment actually does with it. A hook answered in passing, or dissolved within seconds, is a false promise — re-pair the hook with a real payoff or cut it.
- [ ] "The immunity test": list the exit hook types in order. Two of the same type in a row is a warning; three is a rewrite.
- [ ] "The invention test": every pressure, opponent, and plant above must trace to the prose/idea. Staged pressure the material does not contain is fabrication, not dramaturgy — drop it.

## 2d. Attention design (idea mode only — `attention-hooks.md`)

- [ ] One engine is committed (`awe` / `humor` / `conflict`) and the beat pattern matches it, not just the label; the pipeline records the same commitment as `creativeStrategy` and a mismatch between commitment and frames is a rewrite trigger.
- [ ] "The predictability test": name what a viewer of the first two seconds expects (the archetype's default image or the obvious development); if the final frame is a synonym of that expectation, the subversion never landed.
- [ ] Exactly one signature frame exists — retellable in one sentence, aimed at by every other beat, and the exit is that frame or its immediate aftershock. Two equally strong candidates means none was chosen.
- [ ] The subversion lands as a visible state change; muting the video must not remove the surprise (nor, for humor, the laugh).
- [ ] *(humor)* Every character plays it straight — no winking, no comic mugging; the payoff is in a frame, followed by exactly one deadpan reaction beat; no menace or adversarial events were smuggled in to carry a joke the mismatch must carry.
- [ ] *(awe)* The carrier is a deliberate choice among families (megastructure / celestial / living colossal / frozen phenomenon / spatial-law anomaly), not this archetype's most famous symbol; a scale reference keeps the excess measurable.
- [ ] "The re-skin test" (cross-work): compare the signature frame to recent outputs' one-sentence pictures; a synonym is a re-skin — re-derive the anchor keeping the theme, changing the logic.

## 3. Time budget (beats / shots / voice)

- [ ] Beat layer: each segment carries one main beat (1-3 beats); no segment is a beat-dump whose shots all shrink proportionally.
- [ ] Shot layer: per-shot budget = segment duration ÷ shot count; a 10-15s segment holds 2-3 shots (≤ ~3 cuts), and no shot is left under ~3 seconds without a deliberate reason.
- [ ] Voice layer: the segment's total spoken time (Chinese ≈ 4-5 chars/s, English ≈ 2.5-3 words/s; voiceover included) fits inside the segment duration with roughly a fifth left for reaction and visual breathing.
- [ ] Overrunning lines are compressed (information points and causal semantics kept) or moved to a neighbouring segment — never left to be truncated or rushed by the generator.
- [ ] Dialogue-free segments are self-sufficient picture designs (environmental performance, readable on-screen text, atmosphere beat) — no dead air.

## 4. Language fidelity

- [ ] Dialogue preserves original language in `<d>` tags with speaker IDs `(S1)`, `(S2)`…
- [ ] Compressed lines keep the prose's facts and causality; no invented lines or merged speakers.
- [ ] Inner monologue appears as flashback or spoken/voiceover content, never as an empty "thinking" shot.
- [ ] On-screen text (signs, labels) is quoted verbatim in its original language.

## 5. Reference and continuity

- [ ] Shared preset subjects are referenced by label, never redefined inside a segment.
- [ ] New subjects are numbered continuously after the shared maximum and are used in the body.
- [ ] Character appearance matches the shared baseline in every segment (clothing, hair, props).
- [ ] Cut times are strictly increasing, inside the segment duration, and `[Shot 1]` carries no timestamp.
- [ ] The last cut leaves time for the action to land; nothing is scheduled past `durationSeconds`.

## 6. Sound and music

- [ ] `overall_soundscape` covers ambience and physical sounds only (no dialogue repetition).
- [ ] `non_diegetic_music` describes instrumentation/tempo/dynamics and aligns swells or cuts with picture moments.
- [ ] Per-shot synchronized sounds live in the detailed description.

## 7. Format

- [ ] Six sections in canonical order when definitions exist; five when the segment reuses shared subjects only.
- [ ] Summary carries the bracketed task-type prefix (`[reference generation]`…).
- [ ] Genre visuals are concrete and shootable (color, motion, location), consistent with the established visual system.

Mechanical subset automated by the pipeline: cut-point timing and spoken-line budget overruns are surfaced as hints in the generation result (advisory only — nothing blocks display under the zero-blocking contract); structural observations (labels, timing, dialogue tags) join the same hint list for human review.
